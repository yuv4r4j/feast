PRAGMA, drawn: how the model works, what pragmatiq implements, and what an enterprise build needs

PRAGMA is Revolut's foundation model for banking event histories. pragmatiq is an open-source project that says it follows the paper faithfully. This guide walks through the model in pictures, checks that claim against the paper and the pragmatiq source, then lays out what is still yours to build.

Embedding probe

Frozen backbone. Standard-scaled embeddings, logistic regression fitted with L-BFGS. The paper also tries the last [EVT] vector and both together.

LoRA fine-tune

Rank 8, alpha 8, on attention QKV and MLP layers, with Adam. About one-eighth of pre-training time.

Findings worth telling the team

  • Small is already strong for recurring payments and lifetime value. Size pays off most in credit.
  • Adding the profile branch to an events-only PRAGMA-S lifted credit PR-AUC by 31.8% and fraud recall by 85.6%.
  • Dropping the newest events at scoring time moved fraud by at most 0.2% and credit by at most 0.9%. A lagging event feed is tolerable.
Anti-money-laundering: −47.1% F0.5 against the production model, using a linear probe on PRAGMA-L.

1 · The idea

A customer is a timeline of events

Today most banks build one feature pipeline per model: credit has its features, fraud has its own, marketing a third set.

PRAGMA starts from the raw material. Card payments, app screens, trades and messages all share one shape: a timestamp plus key–value pairs.

Plus a snapshot of who they are

The profile holds slow-changing facts at the evaluation point: plan, region, balance band. Nothing after that point is seen.

Life-long items such as the first top-up keep their own date, so old milestones survive when a long history is cut short.

One model reads all of it

PRAGMA turns the events and the profile into one vector that describes the customer: 192 to 1,024 numbers, depending on model size.

Many tasks share that one vector

Each downstream team trains a small model on the embedding, in place of its own feature pipeline.

The paper's corpus gives a sense of the scale involved.

2 · Tokens and time

Every field becomes a key and a value

A language model would read amount: 23.40 as a handful of text fragments and lose the fact that it is a number attached to a field called amount.

PRAGMA keeps the structure. Each field name is a key: one token that says what the field means. The paper has about 60 of them.

Values become tokens by type

A category is one token. A number goes into a percentile bucket learned from the data, with zero in a bucket of its own. Free text is split into pieces, and the key is repeated for each piece.

Key and value are looked up in the same table and added together. This event becomes 10 key–value pairs. The paper's value vocabulary is about 28,000 tokens.

Time is a position, with two clocks

The first clock is seconds before the customer's latest event, squeezed by 8·ln(1+t/8). One minute lands at 17, one year at 122. Recent gaps stay sharp and years of history still fit.

The second is the calendar: hour, weekday and day of month, so daily and weekly rhythm is visible. It applies to events only.

3 · Three encoders

Each event is read alone

The Event Encoder turns one event's tokens into a single vector, the [EVT] output. Events cannot see each other at this stage. Calendar features are added on the way out.

The profile is read alone too

The Profile State Encoder produces one [USR] vector. Its sense of position is the time since each milestone, so "opened four years ago" is something it can use.

Then the whole history is read together

The History Encoder takes [USR] followed by every [EVT], oldest first, with time-to-latest-event as position. Patterns across weeks and months appear here.

It sees one position per event. A 6,500-event history costs 6,501 positions, where the raw tokens would number about 56,000.

One vector comes out

The [USR] output is the customer embedding. All three encoders are bidirectional, pre-norm, with GELU and dropout 0.1.

The same design comes in three sizes. The drawing shows the small one.

4 · Pre-training

Training is a fill-in-the-blank game

No labels are needed. Hide 15% of values at random. The model predicts each one from the rest of its event.

Hide whole events

10% of events are blanked entirely. Now the only clue is the surrounding history.

Hide one field everywhere

10% of keys are blanked across all of the customer's events. To guess an amount, the model has to use everything that is not an amount.

Add a little noise, then score

A few picks become [UNK] and carry no loss, which stops the model leaning on the mask symbol.

Each guess is built from three vectors (the token, its event, the customer) and scored against the same embedding table used on the way in.

5 · Scale plumbing

Histories are wildly uneven

Ordinary batching pads every event to the longest one. Here 25 of 60 cells are padding, and the GPU would spend that share of its time on nothing.

So nothing is padded

All tokens are packed end to end, and attention is told where each event starts and stops. The paper reports 2 to 5 times the throughput of a padded baseline.

Three limits keep memory bounded. The 24-token limit touches only 0.01% of events.

Storage and the training run

Customers with the same number of events are stored together, so the History Encoder batch is a clean rectangle too.

The paper gives hardware and wall-clock time. It does not give learning rates, batch size, warm-up or number of steps. Anyone reimplementing it has to choose these.

6 · Using it

Probe: minutes

Freeze the model, export the embedding, fit a linear model on it. Use this to find out quickly whether a task or a new data source is worth the effort.

LoRA: hours to days

Train small rank-8 adapters on 2 to 4% of the weights. One shared backbone carries a different adapter for each task.

What Revolut reported

Gains of the LoRA-adapted model over their own task-specific models, by size. Credit improves most with scale; recurring payments barely move.

These are relative numbers on private data. They describe Revolut's baselines and are no forecast for yours.

Three findings to plan around

The ablations in the paper say which design choices matter and how robust the model is to a late event feed.

Where it loses

Anti-money-laundering depends on who pays whom. PRAGMA reads each customer in isolation. Plan AML as a separate, graph-based project.

7 · Fidelity check

What matches the paper

Checked by reading the pragmatiq 1.1.0 source against the paper: architecture, tokenisation, masking, prediction head, limits and optimiser all line up. Recounting the layers gives 9.1M, 94M and 940M parameters.

What the paper never said

Learning rates, warm-up, noise rate, bucket counts. pragmatiq picks a default for each and marks it # GUESS in the code. Sweep these first, on the small model.

What differs

Mostly evaluation defaults. Run the probe with --probe-model logistic to follow the paper, pin code-like columns as categories, and expect to write event-level heads yourself.

The verdict

The recipe is followed closely. The results are unproven, and they cannot be proven from outside: the paper publishes relative gains only. Your own out-of-time test is the evidence that counts.

8 · Enterprise build

Both ends are yours

Sources, preparation, export and the consuming models have no code in pragmatiq.

The middle exists, with limits

Tokenising runs on one machine. Multi-GPU training was tested to 8 GPUs, where the paper used 16 to 32. Only a customer-level task head ships.

Governance spans everything

Model-risk validation, fairness checks, explanations and access control on embeddings are left to whoever trains on real data. Bring those teams in from the first real-data stage.

Gaps to close before production

Found by reading the code and its own documentation. None is a reason to stop; each needs an owner.

9 · Rollout

Stage 0: learn on synthetic data

pragmatiq ships a synthetic bank. Use it so the whole team can trace one customer from raw event to embedding without touching real data.

Stage 1: small model, one task

The first real evidence. Keep it narrow: one task, an out-of-time test, and the production model as the bar.

Stage 2: scale and widen

Move to the medium model and several tasks only after stage 1 passes. Repeat the paper's profile and event-lag tests on your data.

Stage 3: production

The stages are a suggested sequence, sized from what the paper reports and what the code supports. Adjust them to your team.

Words your team will hear

evaluation point
The moment a prediction is made. The model sees only what happened before it.
profile state
Slow-changing facts about the customer at the evaluation point.
life-long event
A first-time milestone kept in the profile with its own date.
[USR] and [EVT]
Extra tokens at the front of the profile and of each event. Their outputs summarise the customer and the event.
masked modelling
Training by hiding tokens and predicting them. Needs no labels.
RoPE
A way of telling attention how far apart two positions are. Here the distance is time.
linear probe
A simple linear model fitted on frozen embeddings.
LoRA
Small trainable matrices beside frozen weights, a few percent of the model per task.
token budget
The number of tokens allowed in one batch.
shard
One file of tokenised customers with similar history lengths.
